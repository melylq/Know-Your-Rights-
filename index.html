Freedom of Speech & Expression

Learn about students' rights to express their opinions and ideas at school.

First Amendment

What is it?

The First Amendment protects freedom of religion, speech, the press, peaceful assembly, and the right to petition the government.

What does it protect?

It protects people's right to express their opinions and ideas from government restrictions.

What does it say about freedom of speech?

It protects people's right to express their opinions and ideas from government restrictions.

Official Source

U.S. Constitution — First Amendment | Library of Congress / Congress.gov

Tinker v. Des Moines

What happened?

Students in a public school in Iowa planned to wear black armbands at school as a silent protest against the Vietnam War. The principal warned the students that they would be suspended if they wore the armbands because the protest might cause a disruption in the learning environment.

What did the Supreme Court decide?

In a 7–2 decision in favor of the students, the Supreme Court said that students do not lose their freedom of speech and expression simply because they are at school.

What does this mean for students?

Students have the right to express their opinions at school, but that right is not unlimited. Schools can take action when student expression causes, or meets the legal standard for, a substantial disruption to the school environment.

Official Source

Facts and Case Summary — Tinker v. Des Moines | United States Courts

Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L. (2021)

What happened?

B.L. was a high school student who posted an angry message on Snapchat outside of school after she did not make the school's varsity cheerleading team. The school punished her for the post.

What did the Supreme Court decide?

The Supreme Court ruled in favor of B.L., deciding that the school violated her First Amendment rights by punishing her for the off-campus speech.

What does this mean for students?

Students generally have more freedom of speech when they are outside of school, including when they post online. However, schools can still have some authority over certain serious situations involving off-campus speech.

Official Source

Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L. | Oyez

Privacy & Search

Learn about students' rights to privacy and what schools can and cannot search at school.

4th Amendment

What is it?

The Fourth Amendment is part of the U.S. Constitution and protects people from unreasonable searches and seizures.

What does it protect?

It protects people, their homes, personal belongings, and other property from unreasonable searches and seizures.

How does it relate to students?

Because public schools are government institutions, the Fourth Amendment can protect students from certain unreasonable searches at school. However, school searches have different rules from searches by police.

Official Source

U.S. Constitution — Fourth Amendment | Constitution Annotated, Congress.gov / Library of Congress

New Jersey v. T.L.O. (1985)

What happened?

T.L.O. was a 14-year-old student at a public high school. A teacher reported that she was smoking in the school bathroom. A school administrator searched her purse and found cigarettes, rolling paper, and other items. The search eventually led to evidence of marijuana.

What did the Supreme Court decide?

The Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the Fourth Amendment applies to public school officials. However, the Court decided that the search of T.L.O.'s purse did not violate the Fourth Amendment because the school administrator had reasonable suspicion and the search was reasonable under the circumstances.

What does this mean for students?

Students have privacy rights at school, but those rights are not exactly the same as they are outside school. School officials do not generally need a warrant or probable cause to search a student. Instead, the search must be reasonable and justified by the circumstances.

Official Source

Facts and Case Summary — New Jersey v. T.L.O. | United States Courts

Safford Unified School District v. Redding (2009)

What happened?

Savana Redding was a 13-year-old student. School officials suspected she had prescription pills at school. They searched her backpack and clothes, but then went further and conducted a much more intrusive search. No pills were found.

What did the Supreme Court decide?

The Supreme Court decided that the more intrusive search violated Savana Redding's Fourth Amendment rights. The school had enough suspicion to search her backpack and outer clothing, but not enough suspicion to justify the more intrusive search.

What does this mean for students?

Schools can search students when they have reasonable suspicion, but the search must be reasonable and related to what they are looking for. The more intrusive the search, the stronger the justification needs to be.

Official Source

Safford Unified School Dist. #1 v. Redding | 557 U.S. 364 (2009) | Justia U.S. Supreme Court Center

M-DCPS Policy

What does M-DCPS say?

M-DCPS recognizes that students have a right to privacy and protection from unreasonable searches and seizures under the Fourth Amendment. However, this right is balanced with the school's responsibility to protect students' health, safety, and welfare.

When can school officials search a student's belongings?

School officials may search a student's person or property when they have reasonable suspicion that the student has or is hiding something prohibited by law, school policy, or the Code of Student Conduct.

This can include:

  • 🎒 Book bags
  • 👜 Purses
  • 📱 Devices
  • 🚗 Vehicles
  • 🔒 Lockers
  • Personal belongings

What does this mean for students?

Students have privacy rights at school, but those rights have limits. If school officials have reasonable suspicion that a student has something prohibited, they may search the student's belongings or property.

M-DCPS: Internet & Computer Use

What does M-DCPS say?

M-DCPS encourages students to use computers, networks, the Internet, and email for educational purposes. However, students are responsible for using technology appropriately and following the district's rules.

What online behavior is not allowed?

M-DCPS lists several examples of prohibited technology use, including:

  • Sending obscene or inappropriate material.
  • Making threats against people or the school.
  • Sending electronic messages that threaten physical harm or may be considered cyberbullying.
  • Hacking into school computer systems without authorization.
  • Damaging or interfering with school computer systems.
  • Using school technology without authorization.

What does this mean for students?

Students can use technology and online resources for educational purposes, but they must follow school rules. Certain online actions, especially threats, cyberbullying, hacking, or damaging school systems, can result in disciplinary action.

Discipline & Suspension

Learn about disciplinary strategies, suspension, and the rights students and parents have during the disciplinary process.

M-DCPS: Formal Corrective Strategies

What types of discipline can M-DCPS use?

M-DCPS recognizes several corrective strategies, including:

  • Temporary removal from class: A disruptive student may be temporarily moved to another setting.
  • Permanent removal from class: A student may be removed when their behavior significantly interferes with teaching or other students' learning.
  • Indoor suspension / SCSI: A student may be assigned to a supervised school program instead of being suspended from school.
  • Alternative Intervention Counseling Center: A student may be assigned to counseling as an alternative to suspension in certain situations.

What rights do students have?

When a student is permanently removed from a class, their parent/guardian must be informed and given an opportunity to communicate with the Placement Review Committee. Students with disabilities also have additional protections involving their IEP.

What does this mean for students?

M-DCPS has different disciplinary options besides simply sending a student home. The type of discipline can depend on the student's behavior and the circumstances.

Official Source

Code of Student Conduct — Secondary | Miami-Dade County Public Schools

M-DCPS: Outdoor Suspension

When can a student be suspended?

A principal may suspend a student for 1–10 days for persistent disobedience or serious misconduct. Suspension may be used when other corrective strategies have been considered or are not appropriate.

When can outdoor suspension be used?

  • The student's presence creates a physical danger to themselves or others.
  • A temporary "cooling off" period is needed.
  • The student or parent/guardian refuses an alternative to suspension.

What rights does a suspended student have?

  • The student can request make-up assignments for missed work.
  • The student must be told the reason for the suspension.
  • The student has an opportunity to be heard by the principal or designee.
  • The parent/guardian must receive written notice within 24 hours.

Official Source

Code of Student Conduct — Secondary | Miami-Dade County Public Schools

M-DCPS: Safeguards for Students & Parents/Guardians

What rights do students have?

Students should have an opportunity to explain the circumstances of an incident before corrective action is taken. Students also cannot be disciplined for using a language other than English or because of a disability.

What should schools do?

School administrators should communicate with parents/guardians when corrective strategies are taken against a student.

What can students and parents do if they disagree?

They can use formal complaint procedures, including:

  • A school-level hearing
  • An appeal to the regional center office
  • For alternative education assignments and expulsions, a hearing before an impartial hearing officer

Bullying, Harassment & Safety

Learn about protections against bullying and harassment and what students can do if they experience or witness it.

Model Policy Against Bullying and Harassment

What is bullying and harassment?

Bullying includes repeated behavior that causes physical or psychological harm or creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive school environment. It can include teasing, social exclusion, threats, intimidation, stalking, physical violence, theft, harassment, humiliation, or destruction of property.

Harassment includes threatening, insulting, or dehumanizing behavior that causes fear, interferes with a student's education, or disrupts the school.

Where does the policy apply?

The policy applies at school, during school activities and events, on school buses, and through technology used at school. It can also apply to bullying that happens outside of school if it substantially interferes with a student's ability to participate in or benefit from school or seriously disrupts the educational process.

What can students do if they experience or witness bullying or harassment?

Students can report bullying or harassment to the principal or the principal's designee. Reports can be made in person or anonymously. School employees are required to report suspected bullying or harassment.

After a report is made, the school must investigate the incident, protect the student, and notify the parents or guardians of the students involved.

Official Source

Model Policy Against Bullying and Harassment — Rule 6A-1.0018, F.A.C. | Florida Department of Education

Jeffrey Johnston Stand Up for All Students Act

What does the law prohibit?

Florida law prohibits bullying and harassment of students and school employees in public K–12 schools. It also includes cyberbullying and certain bullying that happens outside of school when it substantially interferes with a student's education or disrupts the school.

What protections and procedures does it require?

School districts must have policies against bullying and harassment. These policies must include ways to report incidents, procedures for investigating reports, parent notification, counseling referrals, and protections for students who report bullying in good faith.

What does this mean for students?

Students have the right to attend school without being bullied or harassed. If bullying or harassment happens, students can report it through the procedures established by their school district.

Official Source

Chapter 1006, Section 147 — Florida Statutes | The Florida Senate

M-DCPS prohibits bullying, harassment, and intimidation by students, school employees, visitors, and volunteers. The district is committed to providing a safe learning environment and does not tolerate bullying or harassment.

Right to Learn & Education

Learn about students' rights to access a safe, equal, and high-quality public education.

Florida Law — Florida Statutes §1002.20

What does Florida law say about a student's right to education?

Florida law states that K–12 public school students have the right to a safe, secure, efficient, and high-quality education.

What does Florida law say about school attendance?

Florida law requires students to attend school according to the state's compulsory attendance laws.

What educational resources do students have the right to receive?

Students have the right to sufficient instructional materials for core subjects, including mathematics, language arts, social studies, science, reading, and literature.

What type of classroom environment do students have the right to have?

Students have the right to an orderly and disciplined classroom that is conducive to learning.

Official Source

Chapter 1002, Section 20 — Florida Statutes | The Florida Senate

Brown v. Board of Education (1954)

What happened?

In Brown v. Board of Education, a Black student named Linda Brown had to travel to a segregated school instead of attending the white school that was closer to her home. Her family challenged the law that allowed public schools to be separated by race, and the case went to the Supreme Court.

What did the Supreme Court decide?

In 1954, the Supreme Court unanimously decided that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional because it violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

What does this mean for students?

This decision helped establish that students cannot be separated into public schools based on race. It was an important step toward equal educational opportunities for all students.

Official Source

Brown v. Board of Education (1954) | National Archives

Plyler v. Doe (1982)

What happened?

Official Source

Plyler v. Doe | 457 U.S. 202 (1982) | Justia U.S. Supreme Court Center

What happened?

Texas passed a law that allowed school districts to deny enrollment to children who were not legally admitted into the United States. A group of children challenged the law because they were being denied access to public education.

What did the Supreme Court decide?

In 1982, the Supreme Court ruled that the Texas law violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court decided that undocumented children are still “persons” protected by the Constitution and cannot be denied a public education based only on their immigration status.

What does this mean for students?

This decision means that public schools cannot deny children access to a basic public education because of their immigration status. It protects equal access to education for students who live within a state.

Official Source

Plyler v. Doe | 457 U.S. 202 (1982) | Justia U.S. Supreme Court Center
